# Ep 3 · Negative energy is real, and we've measured it (Sun Nov 8, 12 PM CT)

Negative energy sounds made up. But we've measured it.

Hey, it's Lola. Last week, we learned a wormhole needs negative energy to stay open. So... does it exist?

Start with empty space. It isn't really empty. Quantum physics says it's fizzing with tiny fluctuations, waves of energy flickering in and out everywhere.

In 1948, Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir had an idea. Put two metal plates very close together in a vacuum. Between them, only the waves that fit the gap survive. Outside, every wave fits. So there's less energy between the plates than in empty space itself. Less than nothing. Negative energy. And the extra push from outside squeezes the plates together.

In 1997, Steve Lamoreaux measured that push. A year later, another team pinned it down to about one percent. The Casimir effect is real.

But here's the catch. It's tiny. The plates have to sit about a micrometer apart, thinner than a single bacterium. And to prop open a wormhole just one meter wide, some estimates say you'd need negative energy equal to the mass of Jupiter.

In sci-fi, they just flip a switch.

So here's my question: If negative energy is real, could we ever make enough of it?
